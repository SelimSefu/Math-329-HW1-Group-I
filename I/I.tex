\section*{Question 1}

Let \(s_i=1-2y_i\). For \(\lambda>0\), the objective function is
\[
f_\lambda(\theta)
=
\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{m}
\phi\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)}_{f(\theta)}
+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2,
\qquad \theta\in E.
\]
We first show that \(f\) is convex. The smoothed hinge loss \(\phi\) satisfies
\[
\phi'(z)=
\begin{cases}
0, & z\leq -1,\\
1+z, & -1<z<0,\\
1, & z\geq 0.
\end{cases}
\]
The values and piecewise derivatives agree
at \(z=-1\) and \(z=0\). Thus \(\phi\) is continuously differentiable.
Its derivative is nondecreasing, so for any \(a,b\in\mathbb R\),
\[
\phi(b)-\phi(a)-\phi'(a)(b-a)
=\int_a^b \bigl(\phi'(u)-\phi'(a)\bigr)\,du\geq 0.
\]
(The inequality holds for either ordering of \(a\) and \(b\).)
By Theorem~4.21 of the lecture notes, \(\phi\) is convex.

For any \(\theta,\eta\in E\) and \(t\in[0,1]\), linearity of the inner
product and convexity of \(\phi\) give
\begin{align*}
\phi\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,(1-t)\theta+t\eta\rangle\right)
&=\phi\!\left((1-t)s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle
+t s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\eta\rangle\right)\\
&\leq (1-t)\phi\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)
+t\phi\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\eta\rangle\right).
\end{align*}
Summing over \(i\) yields
\(f((1-t)\theta+t\eta)\leq(1-t)f(\theta)+tf(\eta)\);
hence \(f\) is convex.

Now
\[
f_\lambda(\theta)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2=f(\theta)
\]
is convex. Since \(\lambda>0\), Definition~4.5 of the lecture notes therefore implies that \(f_\lambda\) is \(\lambda\)-strongly convex.
It is real valued on the whole Euclidean space \(E\), so Theorem~4.28 applies: \(f_\lambda\) has a unique global minimizer \(\theta^\star\).

Moreover, \(f_\lambda\) is convex and differentiable on \(E\).
Each loss term is a composition of the continuously differentiable
function \(\phi\) with a linear map, and the quadratic term is differentiable. By Corollary~4.23 of the lecture notes, a point is
a global minimizer if and only if its gradient vanishes. So, what we can say about the critical points is that
\[
\{\theta\in E:\nabla f_\lambda(\theta)=0\}=\{\theta^\star\}.
\]

\section*{Question 2}

We now set \(\lambda=0.005\). Write
\(g_i(\theta)=s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\).
Each \(g_i:E\to\mathbb R\) is linear, hence differentiable, and \(\phi\) is continuously differentiable as shown in Question 1.
Thus \(f=\sum_{i=1}^m\phi\circ g_i\) is differentiable. By additivity of the differential and the chain rule, for any \(h\in E\),
\[
Df(\theta)[h]
=\sum_{i=1}^m \phi'(g_i(\theta))\,Dg_i(\theta)[h].
\]
Using the limit expression for the differential of \(g_i\), we obtain
\[
Dg_i(\theta)[h]
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{g_i(\theta+th)-g_i(\theta)}{t}
=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{t s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,h\rangle}{t}
=s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,h\rangle.
\]
Substituting and using bilinearity of the inner product gives
\begin{align*}
Df(\theta)[h]
&=\sum_{i=1}^m
s_i\phi'\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)
\langle\widetilde{x}_i,h\rangle\\
&=\left\langle
\sum_{i=1}^m s_i\phi'\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)
\widetilde{x}_i,\ h
\right\rangle.
\end{align*}

The quadratic term \(r(\theta)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2\) is also differentiable. Indeed,
\[
r(\theta+h)-r(\theta)-\lambda\langle\theta,h\rangle
=\frac{\lambda}{2}\|h\|^2=o(\|h\|),
\]
so \(Dr(\theta)[h]=\lambda\langle\theta,h\rangle\). Consequently, \(f_\lambda=f+r\) is differentiable and
\[
Df_\lambda(\theta)[h]
=\left\langle
\sum_{i=1}^m s_i\phi'\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)
\widetilde{x}_i+\lambda\theta,\ h
\right\rangle.
\]
By Definition~2.18 of the lecture notes,
\(Df_\lambda(\theta)[h]=\langle\nabla f_\lambda(\theta),h\rangle\).
Since this equality holds for every \(h\in E\), we conclude that
\[
\boxed{
\nabla f_\lambda(\theta)
=\sum_{i=1}^m
s_i\phi'\!\left(s_i\langle\widetilde{x}_i,\theta\rangle\right)
\widetilde{x}_i+\lambda\theta.
}
\]
\paragraph{Numerical agreement and runtime.}
We checked the objective values and each gradient component using\texttt{np.testing.\allowbreak assert\_allclose}, with absolute and relative
tolerances of \(10^{-10}\). Both implementations agreed within thesetolerances. We timed them on identical inputs using \texttt{timeit.repeat},
retaining the minimum of three single evaluations for each implementation. In this run, vectorization was approximately 18 times faster for the
objective and 14 times faster for the gradient.

\section*{Question 6}

Consider the fixed-step gradient descent iteration
\[
\theta_{k+1}
=
\theta_k-\alpha\nabla f_\lambda(\theta_k),
\qquad k\geq 0.
\]

From Question 1, \(f_\lambda\) is \(\lambda\)-strongly convex since
\[
f_\lambda(\theta)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2=f(\theta)
\]
is convex. Moreover, as stated in the homework,
\[
\nabla f_\lambda
\]
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\[
L=\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+\lambda.
\]
Hence the assumptions of Theorem~4.31 are satisfied with
\[
\mu=\lambda,
\qquad
L=\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+\lambda.
\]

Therefore \(f_\lambda\) has a unique minimizer \(\theta^\star\), and
for the step size
\[
\boxed{
\alpha=\frac{1}{L}
=
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+\lambda},
}
\]
Theorem~4.31 gives
\[
f_\lambda(\theta_k)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\leq
\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{L}\right)^k
\left(
f_\lambda(\theta_0)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\right).
\]
Thus the objective values converge globally and at least linearly to
the optimum.

More generally, for every
\[
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{L},
\]
the same theorem may be applied with Lipschitz constant \(1/\alpha\),
yielding
\[
f_\lambda(\theta_k)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\leq
(1-\alpha\lambda)^k
\left(
f_\lambda(\theta_0)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\right).
\]

For the value \(\lambda=0.005\), the admissible step sizes are therefore
\[
\boxed{
0<\alpha\leq
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+0.005}.
}
\]

\subsection*{Application to our implementation}

Our implementation uses exactly
\[
\alpha=\frac{1}{L},
\qquad
L=\sigma_{\max}(X)^2+\lambda,
\]
so Theorem~4.31 applies directly.

Writing
\[
\kappa=\frac{L}{\lambda},
\]
we obtain
\[
f_\lambda(\theta_k)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\leq
\left(1-\frac{1}{\kappa}\right)^k
\left(
f_\lambda(\theta_0)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\right).
\]

Corollary~4.32 further gives
\[
\|\theta_k-\theta^\star\|
\leq
\sqrt{
\frac{2}{\lambda}
\left(
f_\lambda(\theta_0)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\right)
}
\left(
\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\kappa}}
\right)^k
\]
and
\[
\|\nabla f_\lambda(\theta_k)\|
\leq
\sqrt{
2L
\left(
f_\lambda(\theta_0)-f_\lambda(\theta^\star)
\right)
}
\left(
\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\kappa}}
\right)^k.
\]
Hence both the iterates and the gradient norm converge at least
linearly.

\subsection*{Comparison with the finite run}

This prediction is consistent with our numerical experiment. The plot
shows \(\|\nabla f_\lambda(\theta_k)\|\) decreasing rapidly at first and
then more slowly, until the stopping tolerance is reached after
approximately \(2.7\times10^4\) iterations.

Corollary~4.32 only provides the geometric upper bound
\[
\|\nabla f_\lambda(\theta_k)\|
\leq Cq^k,
\qquad
q=\sqrt{1-\frac{\lambda}{L}}<1,
\]
rather than requiring the gradient norm to decrease by the same factor
at every iteration. Thus the changes of slope and relatively flat
regions in the plot are compatible with the theory.

Since the numerical run stops once the prescribed tolerance is reached,
it does not show convergence all the way to zero, but its behavior is
consistent with the asymptotic prediction
\[
\|\nabla f_\lambda(\theta_k)\|\to 0.
\]

\subsection*{Q8. Mathematical insight}

The regularization term
\[
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2
\]
makes \(f_\lambda\) \(\lambda\)-strongly convex. Therefore, \(f_\lambda\) has a unique minimizer \(w^\star\), and gradient descent converges to \(w^\star\) from any starting point. In particular, with the step size
\[
\alpha=\frac{1}{L},
\]
where \(L\) is the Lipschitz constant of \(\nabla f_\lambda\), gradient descent converges at least linearly.

\subsection*{Computational insight}

Vectorizing the computations significantly improves the efficiency of
the implementation. Instead of evaluating the objective and gradient
with explicit Python loops, we use matrix operations such as
\[
X^\top(X\theta-y).
\]
These operations are executed by optimized numerical linear algebra
routines and are therefore much faster.

In our experiments, across $30$ trials, the median speedup was approximately $20$ times for the objective and $14$ for the gradient. Since gradient
descent requires many iterations, this speed-up has a substantial impact on the total runtime.
